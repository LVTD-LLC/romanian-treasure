# The Moscow Deposit

Romanian Treasure — evidence-linked case file

Research cutoff: 5 September 2026. No treasure has been located or recovered.

Desk investigation using publicly accessible and legitimately available material. No archive visit, paid acquisition, outreach or physical recovery.

## The brief: find the treasure, not a story about it

The case begins with a misleadingly simple label: Romanian Treasure, confirmed, Moscow, 1917. The deposit was real. The unresolved questions are what remained separately identifiable after Soviet seizure, where each component went, and whether any surviving object can now be connected to that deposit. This investigation has not located the missing bank reserve or an authenticated present-day cache. It has identified a documentary route that is more promising than a physical treasure hunt. [S01] [S35]

The best-supported working explanation in the material inspected is that the monetary reserve survived beyond the revolution, then was progressively absorbed or dispersed through Soviet finance in the early 1920s. That conclusion is provisional: the decisive original inventories and disposal annexes were not independently inspected. Published Russian and Romanian researchers converge on continued accounting traces in the early 1920s, while disagreeing over mechanism, quantities and responsibility. The shared ground is important; it is not a substitute for auditing their documents. [S05] [S07] [S09] [S10]

There are three different meanings of success. Physical identification means finding a surviving object or lot with a defensible chain of custody. Documentary reconstruction means showing where the reserve went, even if coins were spent or melted. Restitution means resolving a property or financial obligation, which need not depend on the original atoms of gold surviving. A record that advances one objective may do little for the others. The website keeps those objectives separate.

No coordinates for an excavation follow from the evidence inspected. The present recommendation is an archive-and-provenance investigation: establish the last reliable inventory, follow the first release, and identify the recipient. For cultural objects, begin with the difference between authenticated shipping and return inventories.

## Open four ledgers before following a train

Ledger one is the National Bank of Romania’s monetary gold: approximately 91.5 metric tonnes of fine gold in the current official claim. Ledger two is royal property, including two cases of Queen Marie’s jewels valued separately. Ledger three is cultural and identity treasure: artworks, manuscripts, ecclesiastical objects, museum collections and archives. Ledger four is other financial and private property, including banknotes, securities, records and deposits. A return, theft or transfer from one ledger is not evidence that another ledger was cleared. [S01] [S03] [S04]

The first shipment is described as 1,738 BNR cases plus two royal cases. The BNR value was 314,580,456.84 gold lei. The second BNR shipment contained 188 cases of mixed assets, but its additional gold value was only 574,523.57 gold lei. The billions of lei assigned to the wider second evacuation are not a weight of bullion. Reading all 188 cases as newly shipped gold, or adding every financial valuation as though it were metal, radically changes the case without evidence. [S03] [S04] [S25]

The inspected modern denomination table is particularly instructive. German marks account for 45.56% of its stated BNR value and Austrian crowns for 29.31%; Romanian caroli account for 0.34%. Those are reconstructed value shares, not independently verified mass shares. The consequence is still clear: a search restricted to coins bearing Romanian designs would miss most of the monetary categories in the published inventory. Equally, the discovery of an ordinary Romanian gold coin abroad would not establish that it belonged to this deposit. [S31]

The table’s value rows and case counts reconcile exactly, but its bag rows add to 13,833 rather than the printed 13,823. This small discrepancy is a useful warning. The inventory should be treated as structured evidence to audit, not as an illustration to copy unquestioningly. The original annex may resolve the difference; guessing which row is wrong would contaminate the reconstruction. [S31]

## The first footprints: protocols, calendars and an eyewitness diary

The first deposit agreement is commonly dated 14/27 December 1916. Contemporary Romanian and Russian dates often used the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar during this period. Modern accounts sometimes normalize dates and sometimes preserve them. A departure in December and arrival in January can therefore describe one journey, not two. Even after conversion, published arrival dates vary. This case file preserves uncertainty rather than creating an exact day unsupported by a reconciled record. [S03] [S25]

The reserve was placed in Kremlin treasury facilities and an inventory protocol followed, conventionally cited as 16 February 1917. For the second shipment, the agreement date, 27 July/9 August, must be distinguished from actual departure. Alexandru Lapedatu’s published Moscow diary records leaving Iași on 28 July Old Style, or 10 August New Style, at 2 p.m. His account is the strongest inspected source for actually accompanying part of the evacuation. [S04] [S11]

Lapedatu names Chișinău and Bender, followed by stops or observations including Cherkasy, Pryluky, Bryansk and Moscow. An earlier planned itinerary in the same diary is not identical to the subsequent entries. A detective map must distinguish the route a traveler expected from the places he records reaching. Our map connects observed places schematically; it is not a reconstruction of the exact rail alignment. Printed diary pages 117–121 correspond to PDF pages 121–125. [S11]

The diary also distinguishes custody inside Moscow. Lapedatu describes cultural cases in an unsatisfactory damp room beneath guard stairs, his attempts to improve storage, and later movement to the Ssudnaia Kazna. The entries for the later move describe trucks, rain, and a painting crate checked and found dry. These are useful condition and handling observations, but they do not show that the BNR gold followed the same room-to-room route. His later count of 142 cases below, 38 on the ground floor and three above refers to his charge, not the entire national deposit. [S11]

In the autumn and early winter, Lapedatu recorded fears about protection and proposals to move material out through an American route. A proposal is not a completed transfer. The diary later leaves the Academy cases in Moscow. This is exactly the kind of distinction lost when a proposed evacuation becomes a legend of treasure secretly arriving overseas. [S11]

## The break in custody was political, not simply a lost train

The January 1918 seizure is not supported only by modern Romanian recollection. Contemporary US diplomatic records describe the rupture with Romania, the gold being held, and requests for Allied intervention. The Soviet declaration treated the gold as unavailable to the Romanian oligarchy while promising to preserve it for the Romanian people. Whatever one concludes about the later dispute, that is a claim of deliberate control, not an admission that the deposit had already vanished without trace. [S12] [S13] [S38] [S01]

The asset categories matter immediately after seizure. Published archival research describes sixteen cases of Romanian banknotes removed in January 1918. That can support a claim about misuse of paper currency; it cannot automatically support a claim that the monetary gold paid for revolutionary operations. Similarly, Pokivaylova quotes a later request discussing Romanian valuables explicitly excluding the gold reserve. Translating all these categories as treasure and then treating them as bullion erases a critical distinction. [S09] [S10]

Political responsibility and physical disposition also require different documents. A seizure order establishes an official decision. A stocktake establishes what was found at a particular inspection, subject to the reliability of the inspection. An outgoing voucher and receiving receipt establish a transfer. A retrospective narrative may connect them, but the connection remains something to test.

## The 1919–1925 trail: the most consequential unresolved audit

Ilie Schipor’s Russian-archive reconstruction challenges stories that the whole reserve was lost with White forces, taken by the Czech Legion, melted in 1918 or used for the Brest-Litovsk settlement. His later written account says the inventory annex of 16 February 1917 and an RGAE report dated 23 May 1921 match sufficiently to show the reserve intact. That is a consequential claim. We read it, but did not obtain and independently reconcile the complete original comparison. [S05] [S07] [S08] [S31]

A report dated 13 December 1924 supplies the most specific route description encountered. Viorica Moisuc reproduces a translation through Schipor’s document 121, pages 339–340. It describes removal of royal brilliants and some banknotes in February 1919, then dispersal of the gold by currency during Denikin’s advance in the autumn. British pounds, Turkish lira and German marks went toward a destination or route rendered Samara–Tashkent; Austrian crowns and some German marks went to Perm; French francs, Romanian money and other currencies remained in Moscow. The passage is a retrospective report, not a set of transport receipts. [S09]

After the evacuated gold returned, the same translated passage says French francs, British pounds and German marks were sent almost entirely abroad at various times under the People’s Commissariat for Finance. Austrian crowns were transferred at different times to the State Bank’s Issue Section. This is unusually useful because it splits the search into an overseas payments trail and a domestic financial-institution trail. It does not name the foreign banks, mint lots or ultimate holders. The report’s original Russian text and annexes are the first things to obtain. [S09]

Schipor’s publisher summary dates the return to Moscow to July 1921 and progressive expenditure from autumn 1921 to early 1925. These dates should not be elevated into our own verified transaction schedule. They are an author’s reconstruction awaiting audit against complete documents. A particularly important test is whether an inventory was a fresh physical count or a carried-forward book balance. The latter can preserve a number after custody has changed. [S05] [S07]

Pokivaylova’s Russian monograph provides a partly convergent, politically different account. On page 132 it records Romanian gold still present at the start of 1922, quoting two different balances from other scholars: 105,102,337.88 and 87,074,501.05 gold roubles. These may reflect different scopes, dates or errors; the inspected passage does not resolve them. Averaging them or converting one into a precise surviving tonnage would manufacture certainty. [S10]

The same page describes the gold as обезличено by 1924 and cites GARF f. 374, op. 28, d. 861, l. 91. A reasonable working translation is depersonalized or no longer separately identified. The passage then distinguishes scholars who infer melting from a more cautious view that only some may have been melted. The word alone therefore does not prove a universal melting operation. It does, however, point toward the erosion of a distinct Romanian-deposit identity in the accounts. [S10]

A further check reached a published Soviet accounting document dated not earlier than 1 February 1922: document 236, printed page 391, cited to RGASPI f. 19, op. 1, d. 492, l. 108. Its web transcription separates usable and defective coins, bullion and scrap, then adds other gold and valuable objects to produce different aggregate totals. No Romanian component is identified. It is evidence of how accounting scope changes a total, not an independent confirmation of either Romanian-specific balance. Adjacent published summaries likewise do not supply the missing Romanian-to-recipient link. [S40]

## The American file: a real allegation and a dangerous coincidence

On 15 August 1921 the Romanian Legation submitted a memorandum to the US State Department stating that some gold then reaching the United States came from the Romanian gold sent to Moscow in 1916 and was being used by Soviet authorities for payments abroad. The published document is FRUS 1921, volume II, document 733, decimal file 871.51/251. This is a genuine contemporary lead, not a modern internet invention. It is also only an allegation: the memorandum provides no named ship, bank, weight, lot number or informant. [S17]

The date creates a tension with Schipor’s summarized autumn-1921 start of expenditure. Several explanations remain possible: the allegation was mistaken, it concerned an earlier disposal, the summary’s periodization is coarse, or the underlying records concern different components. The correct next step is to retrieve the supporting file and compare it with the original disposal records. Moving a date to make the narrative smooth would destroy the very clue that needs investigation. [S17] [S05]

US Treasury and State correspondence from March and April 1921 explains how Soviet-origin gold, title concerns and Swedish official mint stamps were handled. The material supplies a mechanism by which metal could enter international circulation with new marks, and a sequence of institutions whose records might survive. It does not itself identify Romanian gold. A matched Russian dispatch, Swedish mint receiving or melt entry, and Western bank or assay receipt would be much stronger than general discussion of gold laundering. [S14] [S15]

A nearby document is especially tempting. The Carmania carried 7,500 imperial gold roubles consigned by the Bank of England to J. P. Morgan & Co.; on 10 August 1921 the New York Assay Office rejected the tender as Soviet-origin Russian gold. The document is real, but it contains no Romanian provenance. Its location near the Romanian memorandum in FRUS and the closeness of the dates do not join the two cargos. This case file deliberately displays it as a control against a sensational but unsupported conclusion. [S16] [S17] [S33]

For a genuine match, look for a shared shipment identifier, denomination/weight combination, case mark, voucher, account entry or explicit originating deposit. Even a successful financial match may reconstruct where value went without identifying which modern bar contains the original metal. That would still be a meaningful historical result.

## Newspapers: clues must survive their quotation chains

A modern transcription by Ion N. Oprea quotes Glasul Bucovinei no. 465 of 10 July 1920 alleging that gold pledged in Danish banks consisted largely of Romanian 20-lei coins. The newspaper reportedly attributed the information to a paper in Copenhagen. This supplies a narrow date window and a testable claim earlier than the US memorandum. It does not yet supply a bank or a verified consignment. [S22]

The original Romanian issue was not inspected. The LIBRARIA digitized collection and an Arcanum collection were located, but catalogue coverage is not the same as obtaining the exact issue. The first task is to see the original column and neighboring material, then identify the Danish paper and any correction. Repeated publication of one unsourced wire story would remain one allegation, not independent corroboration. [S23]

Further period-press leads in the transcription include reported British negotiations and Soviet offers in 1920. Their value is in locating contemporary assertions and diplomatic timing; they should not be mistaken for transaction receipts. French newspaper discovery also produced a Le Temps issue dated 20 February 1935, but the inspected OCR extract did not yield a verified treasure passage. It was not promoted to evidence. This negative result is retained in the methods log rather than padded into the bibliography as a corroborating source.

## The Boyle and royal-jewels cases need their own inventory

Joseph Boyle’s recovery mission is historical. What he recovered is the question. William Rodney’s Dictionary of Canadian Biography article explicitly describes archives and paper currency, not the bullion and crown jewels claimed in some retellings. Geoffrey Swain’s scholarly article points to valuable archival collections but also repeats a jewel account through George Hill’s memoir. These should be compared, not blended into a single heroic cargo. [S18] [S19]

The translated 1924 report’s account of royal brilliants removed in February 1919 introduces another conflict. It may concern different objects, inaccurate recollection or a mistaken identification; the inspected material cannot decide. Begin with the contents of the two 1916 royal cases, then compare the 1917 cargo and receiving records and the 1919 removal inventory. “The crown jewels” is too broad a label to resolve this. [S09]

Swain cites Boyle papers at PRONI under D1943/1–3 and British diplomatic material. Kent State’s Queen Marie finding aid identifies later correspondence concerning Boyle and the treasure, including 1946 and 1990 material. Those are concrete research paths, but a catalogue entry is not the letter itself, and later correspondence is not automatically eyewitness testimony. Hill’s Go Spy the Land and Romașcanu’s early documentary compilation are located reading targets, not books fully read during this session. [S19] [S20] [S26]

## The returned treasure creates a better cultural-object search

The 1935 and 1956 returns prove that parts of the evacuated property remained identifiable long after the revolution. They do not establish that the monetary reserve was returned. The 1935 handover protocol is reported as covering 1,443 cases and bales plus 27 folders and packages. The route was Moscow by rail to Odesa, then aboard the Principesa Maria to Constanța, then inland to Bucharest. BNR’s 212 cases contained banknotes, printing materials, securities and records. [S03] [S04]

For 1956, the institutional account describes a protocol signed on 6 August and two trains arriving in Bucharest on 14 and 15 August. The headline total is 39,320 objects, including paintings, icons, textiles, historical coins, medals and applied art. The reported component figures add to 39,322, an unresolved two-object discrepancy in that summary. The 33,068 coins are not described as all gold. Gold within returned objects exceeded 33 kg; this is not a return of the 91.5-tonne monetary reserve. [S03]

Pietroasele—the famous Cloșca cu puii de aur—is an example of an identifiable returned treasure. MNIR’s 2016 announcement explicitly links it to the 1956 return and says that National Art Museum registers recording returned objects were exhibited. These registers are more useful for a practical cultural-object search than a generalized count of crates. A missing-object list needs old and new accession numbers, photographs, dimensions, materials, descriptions and institutional owners. [S04]

The cultural track can produce results even if the bank gold lost its identity. First establish that an object was actually shipped; then show it was not among documented returns or later transfers; only then search museum catalogues, provenance records and sale histories. Differences in packing, regrouping and renumbering can make crate totals diverge without proving an object lost. A one-to-one reconciliation is laborious but falsifiable.

## Can twelve coins bridge a century?

Reporting describes a donation to MNIR on 6 March 2008 of twelve French and Belgian gold coins totaling 77.09 grams, connected to a private return story. Reported accession and donation identifiers offer a bounded starting point. We did not obtain the museum’s accession file, the expert report or a treasury-release document. For now this is a provenance lead, not a confirmed surviving sample of the missing reserve. [S24]

There are three separate tests. Are the coins genuine historical objects? Are their dates and types compatible with the original deposit? Is there documentary continuity from that deposit to these particular coins? The first two can be true while the third remains entirely unproved. Common coins without unique identifiers are especially difficult to distinguish after they enter circulation.

The request should therefore seek the donor’s declaration, acquisition circumstances, photographs, expert conclusions and any Russian custody or release record. If the only Moscow connection is the donor’s assertion, the appropriate result is unverified provenance. A small sample must not be used to authenticate a much larger claimed cache by association. Current private-return stories likewise require a verified custodian and itemized inventory before they become a recovery path. [S24] [S30]

## Restitution is a different investigation from locating metal

The deposited property and the guarantees around it are central to Romania’s case. The European Parliament’s 2024 resolution records the 91.5-tonne claim, partial cultural returns and the commission’s acknowledgement of authentic deposit documents with treaty value. The resolution is a political instrument, not a court judgment or a current inspection of Russian vaults. [S01]

Russian official responses and Russian historiography invoke other wartime costs, damage, reparations, debts and transfers. Those are positions that belong in the file. They should not be dismissed as nonexistent, but neither do they prove the specific deposit was returned or legally discharged. A defensible settlement claim needs the actual agreement and itemized accounting, with attention to parties, dates, ownership and the obligation purportedly extinguished. This research has not adjudicated those legal questions. [S10] [S29]

The joint historical commission, established through the 2003 framework and first meeting in 2004, had met five times by the record cited in the 2024 resolution, last in 2019. BNR’s 2025 portal and a proposed strategy reported in April 2026 show continuing activity. Neither is a recovered shipment. A documentary finding about disposal could strengthen historical understanding or a restitution argument without creating a present physical recovery target. [S01] [S02] [S03] [S28]

An accounting detail also deserves care: a BNR-hosted monetary history says the Moscow deposit was removed from the note-cover stock in the 1929 reform. That changes the reserve assets counted as backing for currency. It is not evidence that the gold physically returned or that the separate deposit obligation was legally discharged. [S39]

## The numerical audit: do not silently repair the sources

At the historical parity of 9/31 gram of fine gold per gold leu, the first published value of 314,580,456.84 gold lei corresponds arithmetically to about 91.3298 tonnes, and 574,523.57 corresponds to about 0.1668 tonnes. Their sum is 315,154,980.41 gold lei, or about 91.4966 tonnes, consistent with rounding to 91.5. This is a monetary-parity calculation, not a new physical assay or independent confirmation of each coin’s fineness. [S03] [S31] The parity is derived from 3,100 lei per kilogram of 900‰ mint gold in the BNR-hosted monetary history: 900 grams divided by 3,100 lei = 9/31 gram of fine gold per leu. [S39]

The BNR material reprinted by Adevărul gives a total of 315,179,980.41 gold lei, which is 25,000 more than those two stated component values. Both totals round to approximately 91.5 tonnes at that parity. That makes the modern rounded claim robust to this particular discrepancy, but it does not identify which underlying entry needs correction. The original ledger should decide. [S03]

Adding the royal jewels’ 7-million-gold-lei appraisal to a bullion valuation can produce a gold-equivalent value near some larger published figures. It cannot establish the literal gold weight of the jewels or explain every 93.4/93.452-tonne statement. Nor does a neat numerical resemblance justify treating the 120-tonne popular description as a reconciled gross-weight figure. Asset boundaries and original measurement columns remain necessary. [S25] [S35] [S37]

Other open audit items are the ten-bag difference in the modern first-shipment table, the two-object difference in the published 1956 category totals, the conflicting 1922 rouble balances and the ambiguous numerical expression in the translated 1924 report. Each is shown openly. None is large enough by itself to prove a theft, cover-up or alternate treasure location. [S03] [S09] [S10] [S31]

First, obtain the full Schipor document annexes and the original 1919, May 1921 and December 1924 records, including neighboring folios and attachments. Separately inspect GARF f. 374, op. 28, d. 861, l. 91 and the February 1922 reports cited as RGASPI f. 5, op. 1, d. 1372, ll. 3–8. These shelfmarks are published citations, not confirmations of present access. The objective is a reproducible inventory bridge, not another narrative summary. [S05] [S07] [S09] [S10]

Second, follow the first documented release. For foreign disposal, seek outgoing vouchers, currency groups, weight, recipient, transport and payment account. For Austrian crowns, pursue the distinct Issue Section route. If a lot can be matched across institutions, preserve every identifier before any remelting or reclassification. A table of matched and unmatched transactions is a stronger result than a world map covered in speculative arrows.

Third, retrieve the supporting file for the August 1921 US memorandum. If it contains no further identifiers, record that negative result and reduce the lead’s weight. In parallel, test the bounded MNIR twelve-coin donation and begin a cultural-inventory reconciliation. These smaller projects can establish whether there are genuinely identifiable physical objects to pursue. [S17] [S24] [S04]

Fourth, inspect the original July 1920 newspaper and chase its Danish attribution. Resolve the Boyle cargo discrepancy with actual shipping and receiving records. Use French diplomatic files to fill custody gaps, not to infer control merely from possession of keys. Eastern-hoard searches should wait for a Romanian-specific link into that chain. [S22] [S23] [S18] [S19] [S21] [S10]

The lead cards contain draft requests in English, Romanian, Russian and French, a success condition and a stopping condition. None has been sent. No books were purchased, no paid database access was acquired, and no claim was lodged. The next research phase can be scoped around concrete documents and identifiable objects rather than an open-ended promise to find 91.5 tonnes.

## Methods, limits and what would change this conclusion

This is a desk investigation with a research cutoff of 5 September 2026. It used sources in Romanian, Russian, English and French; Danish terms are search leads, not Danish records read. Full texts obtained include Pokivaylova’s monograph, Lapedatu’s published memoir/diary volume, Moisuc’s article, Swain’s article and a French archive finding aid. Relevant passages were read; possession of a full PDF is not a claim to have read every page. The two Oscar Print book files obtained are short previews, explicitly labeled as such. Published Russian accounting-document transcriptions from the 2018 collection on Soviet confessional policy were also inspected as controls; the relevant entry is registered as S40.

The source register distinguishes primary records, eyewitness publication, retrospective archival reconstruction, official advocacy, scholarly interpretation, reporting, abstracts and catalogues. It also identifies evidence families. Several Romanian pages repeat Schipor or BNR; their number does not make the underlying assertion independently corroborated. US official publication authenticates the existence and content of a diplomatic allegation, not its truth. A catalogue authenticates a discovery path, not the contents of an unread file.

Original archive visits, complete BNR protocol scans, the decisive Russian annexes, paid books, museum accession files and the original July 1920 newspaper remain outside the inspected material. The BNR portal returned a navigation shell during automated retrieval. Direct attempts to retrieve some newspaper material were blocked, and a Mosyakin preview download timed out. Those limits were not bypassed or disguised as successful reading. Raw third-party books and scans remain outside the published site; source links and original summaries are provided instead.

Working translations aim to preserve uncertainty and asset categories; they are not certified translations. OCR and modern transcriptions can introduce errors, especially in tables and historical place names. Date conversions are only asserted when the source calendar is identified. Geographic connections are schematic and source-qualified. No probability percentages have been invented for competing hypotheses.

A current, authenticated inventory tied to the original deposit would materially strengthen the intact-hoard hypothesis. A complete physical stocktake matching 1917 to May 1921 would weaken early total-loss accounts. Matched disposal and receiving records would strengthen the dispersal explanation and potentially identify recipients. A museum object with continuous provenance could produce a concrete physical identification even if the bulk gold is no longer separable. Until such evidence is obtained, the honest conclusion is an unresolved reserve, a promising documentary trail, and a distinct cultural-object recovery opportunity.

### E12: A newspaper clue with three missing links

Unverified period-press allegation

## Complete chronology

### T01: Autumn 1916 — A state retreats; a reserve becomes vulnerable

context / Documented context

Romania’s military reverses drove the government and central bank to Iași. Evacuation was a response to a real threat, not a treasure expedition. Russian pressure and guarantees enter the documentary story before shipment.

Limit: A later Soviet seizure does not by itself establish a single premeditated plan stretching back through the imperial government.

gold / Officially documented

The first shipment comprised 1,738 BNR cases and two cases of Queen Marie’s jewels. The bank’s stated value was 314,580,456.84 gold lei; the jewels were separately valued at 7 million.

Limit: We read published descriptions and a reconstructed table, not the complete original protocol and every annex. Do not turn the jewellery valuation into literal bullion weight.

gold / Officially documented

gold / Officially documented

The inventory and protocol recorded the first shipment’s contents. Later comparisons need its denominations, case numbers, weights and signatures—not merely “Romanian gold” as a label.

Limit: Calendar normalization varies across retellings. The current table is a modern reconstruction; its bag-count discrepancy remains open.

Limit: His charge was a subset of the cultural shipment, not all bank bullion or every institution’s property. Printed diary pp. 117–119.

## Hypothesis tests

### H01: An intact reserve still hidden in Moscow

Weakly supported

Supports: The initial deposit and Soviet retention are well evidenced. Some cultural holdings remained identifiable for decades.

Against / limits: Published Russian-archive research describes movements, foreign disposal and loss of separate identity by the mid-1920s. No inspected current inventory, vault receipt or recent witness establishes an intact 91.5-tonne holding.

Test: A verifiable current stock record linked to the 1917 deposit, supported by independent inspection. A vague “Moscow” label is insufficient.

Sources: S01, S05, S09, S10

### H02: Absorbed and dispersed through Soviet finance

Best-supported working explanation

Supports: The translated 1924 report names overseas disposal by currency and domestic transfers to the Issue Section. Pokivaylova independently discusses 1922 balances and 1924 depersonalization.

Against / limits: Exact disposal dates, complete quantities and recipients remain unaudited here. The full original reports are missing; published authors disagree on balances and mechanism.

Test: Reconcile the 1917, 1919, 1921, 1922 and 1924 records with outgoing vouchers, foreign bank receipts and mint ledgers.

Sources: S05, S07, S09, S10

### H03: Taken with Kolchak’s gold or by the Czech Legion

Romanian linkage unproven

Supports: Imperial Russian gold really moved east and parts were captured, spent or lost. Pokivaylova discusses possible Romanian connections.

Against / limits: Her Romanian linkage is explicitly tentative; the cited Czech report itself relies on material of uncertain origin. No inspected manifest joins these cargos to the Romanian deposit. A sustained 1921 inventory match would weaken an early total loss.

Test: A contemporaneous transfer receipt identifying Romanian case marks or a unique denomination/weight match, not merely foreign coins among Russian gold.

Sources: S10, S07

### H04: All melted or used for Brest-Litovsk payments in 1918

Poor fit with later reported inventories

Supports: Soviet Russia exported gold and reminting could erase identifying features. Those general processes were real.

Against / limits: Later reported Romanian balances and the proposed May 1921 match contradict disappearance of the whole reserve in 1918. General Russian gold exports do not identify Romanian ownership.

Test: Specific 1918 release and mint/payment records that identify the Romanian deposit, reconciled against later balances.

Sources: S07, S09, S10, S14

### H05: Scandinavian processing and Western buyers

Plausible route; Romanian consignments not yet matched

Supports: US records directly describe Swedish-mint gold handling. Romania alleged US-bound disposal in August 1921; a 1920 press claim points to Denmark.

Against / limits: Neither inspected allegation identifies a proven Romanian lot. The Carmania file is Russian-gold evidence only. A mint stamp does not establish prior ownership.

Test: Match a Russian outgoing voucher, Scandinavian receipt/melt number and Western assay or bank entry by date, mass and identifiers.

Sources: S14, S15, S16, S17, S22

### H06: Boyle recovered the reserve or all the royal jewels

Reserve claim unsupported; jewel story disputed

Supports: Boyle’s successful recovery mission is historical, and Hill’s memoir inspired dramatic accounts.

Against / limits: The Dictionary of Canadian Biography explicitly excludes bullion and crown jewels. A later published Russian report places royal brilliants in a 1919 removal. These are not reconciled object lists.

Test: Compare Boyle’s cargo and Romanian receiving lists with the two 1916 royal cases and the 1919 removal inventory.

Sources: S18, S19, S09, S20

### H07: The reserve was already repaid through later returns or offsets

Not demonstrated by inspected records

Supports: Cultural returns occurred in 1935 and 1956; Russian officials invoke other debts, reparations and financial transfers.

Against / limits: Official Romanian inventories and the European Parliament distinguish those returns from the BNR gold. Counterclaims about debt do not themselves identify a reserve repayment or a legally agreed setoff.

Test: An authenticated settlement instrument plus itemized receipt or discharge of this specific deposit obligation.

Sources: S01, S03, S04, S10, S29

### H08: A private intermediary can deliver a surviving cache now

No authenticated current holding found

Supports: There are recurring private-return stories and a reported twelve-coin donation worth testing.

Against / limits: No inspected stock list, independent custodian confirmation or continuous provenance supports an available cache. Old coins and elaborate stories are not enough.

Test: Verify the custodian and inventory first, then documentary provenance and independent inspection. Do not let an unverified sample authenticate a much larger promised holding.

Sources: S24, S30
